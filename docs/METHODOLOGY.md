# EP-LOOP Methodology — Plain-English One-Pager

Oct 2, 2026 · @Ashis

This explains, in plain words, how EP-LOOP decides a stock is doing something unusual and how it checks whether that was worth paying attention to.

See it live: [EP Loop — Daily Episodic Pivots](https://99ashis.github.io/EP-LOOP/)

## 1. What is an Episodic Pivot (EP)?

Most of the time, a business sits at a steady state — earnings growing at a predictable pace, the balance sheet unchanged, the addressable market about the same size as last quarter. The stock price reflects that steady state too.

An **Episodic Pivot** is a day when a stock's price jumps by a lot more than normal, on unusually heavy trading — a signal that the market may be pricing in a possible change to that steady state: an inflection in earnings trajectory, a shift in the balance sheet or leverage profile, or a change in the size of the addressable market (TAM). We call that day the stock's **anchor** — the moment the business may be starting to gain momentum instead of just sitting still.

## 2. The anchor — the starting line

The day a stock "wakes up" becomes its **anchor** — like the starting line of a race. Its closing price that day is the anchor price.

Everything after that is measured as time-since-the-starting-line: 5 days later, 10 days later, 15 days later, 20 days later. We're always asking: *compared to the anchor, how is this stock doing now?*

## 3. Persistent EP vs. Sustained EP

Both describe a stock that keeps showing momentum after the anchor day — evidence that the move isn't a one-day fluke, but in different patterns:

- **Persistent EP** — the stock re-confirms the move again, in a separate later episode, with a quieter stretch in between. The market keeps getting fresh reasons to re-price the business.
- **Sustained EP** — the stock's strength just continues, day after day, without a break. The momentum doesn't let up long enough to even look like separate episodes.

Simple way to remember it: **Persistent = the signal keeps recurring. Sustained = the signal never really stops.**

## 4. The 9 buckets — where a stock lands

At the end of each time window, every stock gets sorted into exactly one of 9 buckets, based on what it actually did:

| # | Bucket | In plain words |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Pure New | Woke up once. Nothing more happened. |
| 2 | Persistent ×1 | Woke up again, one more time. |
| 3 | Persistent ×2 | Woke up again, two more times. |
| 4 | Persistent ×3+ | Woke up again, three or more times. |
| 5 | Sustained ×1 | Stayed strong continuously, for one stretch. |
| 6 | Sustained ×2 | Stayed strong continuously, in two separate stretches. |
| 7 | Sustained ×3+ | Stayed strong continuously, in three or more stretches. |
| 8 | Fizzle | Lost the burst — went quiet or reversed. |
| 9 | Mixed | A bit of everything above, overlapping. |

The buckets that matter most are **3, 4, and 7** — Persistent ×2, Persistent ×3+, and Sustained ×3+. These are where the price behavior is most convincing, and that's exactly why they're what triggers the fundamental research check (Stage 2).

## 5. What "excess return" means

It's not just "did the stock go up" — it's "did the stock do **better than the overall market** over the same days."

- Stock goes up 5%, but the market also goes up 5% → **0% excess**. No real edge — it just moved with everyone else.
- Stock goes up 10%, market only goes up 2% → **+8% excess**. That's genuine outperformance.

We compare every stock against two markets — Nifty 500 (the broad market) and NIFTY MIDSML 400 (mid and small caps, since that's where most of these pivots happen) — so we're not fooling ourselves during a month when everything goes up anyway.

## 6. The four windows, and checking back later

We don't just look once — we set up the race (the anchor) using four different window lengths — **5, 10, 15, and 20 trading days** — run independently on the same stock. Each window gets its own bucket.

Then, however the stock landed in that window's bucket, we check back at fixed later points — **5, 10, 20, and 30 trading sessions after the window's own anchor** — to see what actually happened to the price, and score the excess return each time.

Here's a real, live example — **WELCORP**, which landed in the **5-day window, Persistent ×2** bucket on 27 Aug 2026:

| Checked back at | WELCORP's move | vs Nifty 500 | vs NIFTY MIDSML 400 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 5 sessions later | +9.80% | +10.77% excess | +10.58% excess |
| 10 sessions later | +15.43% | +17.68% excess | +17.12% excess |
| 20 sessions later | +17.73% | +21.47% excess | +21.24% excess |
| 30 sessions later | Pending | Pending | Pending |

Nothing here is backfitted — each of these numbers was scored *after* the fact, on a schedule set before we knew the outcome. That's what "forward-tested, not backtested" means in practice.
